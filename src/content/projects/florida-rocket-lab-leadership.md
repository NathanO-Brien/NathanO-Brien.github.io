## The team I joined

I joined [Florida Rocket Lab](https://floridarocketlab.org) in July 2021, back when it was still called the Liquid Propulsion Development Team. The rebrand hadn't happened yet, and the team was still small. We had a headcount around thirty, and these were the pre-hotfire days. We were working on three projects at once: Leaky Faucet, an early hybrid engine that gave the team its first taste of real chemical combustion; Sparrow, the liquid bipropellant engine that would eventually become the team's flagship effort; and Hopper, a vertical test vehicle that later got renamed Skipper. The team represented a cross-disciplinary group composed of mostly aerospace and mechanical engineers with some computer science, electrical engineering, and business students sprinkled in. The team grew almost entirely through word of mouth, hard projects and a hint of the 'tism.

Florida Rocket Lab was organized into subteams, each with its own director: Engine Design, Manufacturing, Propellant Delivery, Electronics, Testing, Safety, and Simulations, plus Business once I founded it. That structure mandated eight directors to split work and report up to an executive board composed of a President, CTO, and Vice President.

## When the funding disappeared

In early 2022, the University of Florida changed its student organization funding policy in response to a lawsuit. Prior to the change, the UF MAE Department had been funding the team to the tune of about $10,000 a year; after the change, that amount dropped to under $5,000. This drastically affected team morale. Without some type of external funding, such a hit to the budget would have been enough to stall out our projects and halt all of the recruiting momentum. Something had to change, or we were screwed.

That's the reason I founded the Business Development subteam. At the time, I wasn't particular thrilled at the thought of focusing my time on the teams finances, but someone had to do it. I hadn't ever acted in a fundraising capacity, but I knew I could grow into the role. The most pressing issue was the budget shortfall, so thats where I started.

## Building something from nothing

For the first year, all business development activities were run by just me and Jonner Delgado, the team's president at the time. We had to figure out everything from scratch. That meant learning what documents we needed to fundraise,and what administrators we needed to talk to to keep our student government and departmental funding intact. We also had to learn what reps to talk to at commercial aerospace companies and how to broach the topic of sponsorship. In the early days, we failed A LOT, but with every mistake we knew what not to do the next time.

This helped cement the business development subteam as a permanent fixture of Florida Rocket Lab even after the budget deficet had been corrected.

## Advocacy beyond campus

Some of the work was less about fundraising and more about representing the team in unexpected environments. I advised Jonner when he briefly ran for a seat in UF's Student Government on the Change party ticket. My team also traveled to Washington DC and Orlando alongside the executive board to represent the team at industry conferences. As part of these events, we networked heavily with US Space Force and Space Florida. We also presented to a former U.S. House member about commercial space engagement on college campuses and facilitated engine testing opportunities for Florida design teams at Cecil Spaceport.

## Growing the team, growing careers

Much of my role ended up being about cultivating the team's outreach as much as its money. In early 2023, I brought on a dedicated social media manager and we completely remade the team's presence on LinkedIn, Instagram, and Facebook. I also brought on a CS student to develop the team's first website, and successfully lobbied the MAE department to echo team announcements. These efforts drastically increased team headcount and community engagement, allowing us to hit a triple digit headcount by late 2023.

<figure class="narrow">

Throughout my tenure as Business Development Director, I tried my best to organize a culture of mutual assistance inside the team. Other directors were encouraged to host resume reviews before internship season, and veteran members were expected to share internship application tips and tricks. I also put together a master internship booklet highlighting commercial space and defense companies that our members needed to know about. This resource had application links, deadlines, and notes from alumni who'd already worked across industry. It also listed times and venues for getting one-on-one interview preparation help, and allowed newer members to privately ask questions and request resume feedback if they wanted it.

## What it built

Over two years, my subteam brought in roughly $45,000 in sponsorship, split between direct cash donations, in-kind material donations, and free software access. The team was scrappy for money the whole time I was there, so every one of those relationships took real, repeated work to land and keep. ## Where the team is now

I left Florida Rocket Lab at the end of 2024 when I graduated from the University of Florida. The following semester, the team achieved its first real static-fire successes on Sparrow, the engine that had been the team's flagship project since before I joined. The clip below is from that campaign.

From there, the team kept going. Sparrow went on to complete a full-duration, zero-failure hotfire campaign that the University of Florida's own Mechanical &amp; Aerospace Engineering department later recognized as the first of its kind in university history. Then, on May 30, 2026, the team flew Mirage, the first liquid bipropellant rocket in UF history, at the FAR-OUT competition in the Mojave Desert. It reached 7,646 feet, within 6% of its simulated target, and was recovered completely undamaged. Sparrow successfully powered Mirage throughout ascent.

None of FRL's products were built by any one person, and I was long gone by the time anything flew. But the team that pulled it off is the same one my subteam helped keep funded and growing back when it had nothing to show for itself yet. I am extremely proud of the new generation of leadership, and I look forward to supporting the team as an alumnus for years to come.
